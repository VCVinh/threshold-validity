## Abstract

Validation plans for AI-assisted pathology tools routinely state that a decision threshold "was set to achieve 90% sensitivity on the validation set," without stating how many positive cases the validation set contained. We show this omission is not a minor reporting gap: because a threshold set to hit a target sensitivity is, by construction, an order statistic of the validation set's positive-case scores, the sensitivity such a threshold will actually deliver on new data follows an exactly known Beta distribution determined solely by the number of validation positives — independent of any domain shift, scanner effect, or site difference. This distribution is the same finite-sample coverage guarantee underlying split conformal prediction; our contribution is applying it, with tooling, to slide-level sensitivity reporting in computational pathology, a framing and unit of analysis the conformal literature does not itself address. At 8 validation positives, a threshold nominally targeting 90% sensitivity delivers, at 95% confidence, anywhere from 63.1% to 99.7% sensitivity on new data from the identical population. The minimum-$n$ question turns out to be subtler than it looks: the confidence lower bound is not monotone in $n$ — it rises within each block of $n$ sharing the same order-statistic index $k$, then drops back at the start of the next block, a real sawtooth we verify by direct scan rather than assume away. "Minimum $n$" is therefore only a meaningful guarantee if it means the smallest $n$ safe at every larger $n$ too, not merely the first $n$ that happens to pass — a distinction our own first implementation missed (a self-run adversarial re-check of our own draft caught it; see §2.3 and §4, which also reports a second such re-check overturning this paper's own original claim in §3.3). Correctly defined, guaranteeing a defensible lower bound of 85% sensitivity while targeting 90% requires 177 positive validation cases, and 88% requires 976. We further show that at small validation sizes, common target sensitivities are not merely imprecise but literally inexpressible: at 8 positives, the achievable grid of nominal sensitivities has 12.5-percentage-point steps, so "90% sensitivity" cannot be set at all — the nearest achievable values are 87.5% and 100%. We describe a verified, open-source tool implementing this analysis, to be released alongside submission, and demonstrate its use as a pre-screening check that separates sampling-explainable performance shortfalls from shortfalls requiring a substantive explanation. We apply it to an illustrative case from our own prior work, presented as a range rather than a single verdict because its exact validation-set size could not be recovered, where the sampling-alone verdict flips between "fully explained" and "not explained" depending on that size — a dependency the tool exposes explicitly rather than allowing to remain implicit. A second, external application — to a published study with a fully specified calibration count — is reported in §3.3 together with its own retraction: an initial reading of a same-document count discrepancy did not survive a same-day arithmetic re-check, and we report the withdrawal in the same section as the original claim rather than as a quiet correction.

## 1. Introduction

Regulatory guidance for AI-assisted diagnostic and decision-support tools increasingly asks sponsors to characterize model risk as a function of both model influence on the clinical decision and the consequence of an incorrect decision, and to report signed error rates (true/false positive and negative counts) with confidence intervals [FDA AI guidance, Jan 2025]. What this guidance does not currently specify — and what published validation studies in computational pathology routinely omit — is the sample-size dependence of the operating point itself.

A threshold set to achieve a target sensitivity on a validation set is not a fixed, known-accurate cutoff. It is a statistic computed from a finite sample, and like any sample statistic it has a sampling distribution. When that sampling distribution is wide — which it is, at the validation-set sizes commonly used in practice — an observed shortfall between the target and the sensitivity achieved on new data requires no domain-shift explanation at all. We formalize this, quantify it exactly, and show it matters at realistic sample sizes.

**Contributions.**
1. We apply the exact finite-sample coverage distribution underlying split conformal prediction and classical tolerance-interval theory [Vovk et al. 2005; Lei et al. 2018; Wilks 1941] to the specific, currently unaddressed problem of slide-level sensitivity-threshold validation reporting in computational pathology, and verify the resulting closed form against Monte Carlo simulation.
2. We use this to derive the minimum validation-set size required to guarantee a stated lower-bound sensitivity at a stated confidence level, and tabulate it for common targets — a direct, practitioner-facing translation of the underlying theory. Closely related closed-form sample-size work exists from the TRIPOD+AI/PROBAST+AI group (Whittle et al. 2025, §4); this paper's distinct target-versus-estimate framing — the sampling distribution of a threshold set to target a sensitivity, rather than precise estimation of performance at a fixed threshold — is supported by a live run reconciliation (PMVAL reconciliation, filed alongside this draft); full-text formula comparison remains open, with Whittle et al. cited as the related-but-distinct work subject to a substitution hazard: their formula reproduces this paper's first-pass values (34, 144, 884), not the permanent guarantees (48, 177, 976) — confirmed by a live run of pmvalsampsize 0.2.1's recall module at prescribed recall 0.90 (2026-09-12, PMVAL reconciliation §10): tool outputs 35/138/864 positives, matching first-pass within 1/5/19 but undercounting the permanent guarantees by 13/≈39/≈111 positives (≈27%/22%/11%, i.e. 11–27%).
3. We show that small validation sets cannot express arbitrary target sensitivities at all, and characterize the achievable grid.
4. We apply the tool illustratively to a result from our own prior work whose exact validation-set size could not be recovered, showing the sampling-alone verdict flips between "fully explained" and "not explained" across the plausible range of that size — reported as an explicit function of the missing parameter rather than a single claimed verdict.
5. We apply the tool to a published, peer-reviewed external validation study with a fully specified calibration count (§3.3); an initial reading of a same-document count discrepancy is reported alongside the arithmetic re-check that overturned it, as a worked example of the pre-screening step itself catching an over-reach before publication rather than after.
6. We will release open-source, tested software implementing all of the above, and this manuscript's arithmetic is reproducible directly from the numbers quoted in §3.3.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 The threshold as an order statistic

Let a validation set contain $n$ positive cases with scores $s_1, \ldots, s_n$ from a scoring model. A threshold $\tau$ set to achieve nominal sensitivity $t$ on this validation set is the value of the $k$-th smallest of these scores, where $k = \mathrm{round}((1-t) \cdot n)$, and a case is counted as passing when its score exceeds $\tau$ (strict inequality) — so that the $k$ lowest-scoring validation positives are excluded and $(n-k)/n \approx t$ of the validation positives pass. Equivalently, $n-k+1$ validation positives score at or equal to or above $\tau$, of which the $k$-th score ($\tau$ itself) does not count as a pass under the strict-inequality convention used throughout this paper; the achieved-on-validation-set fraction is therefore $(n-k)/n$, not $(n-k+1)/n$ — an earlier draft of this section stated the latter, which is inconsistent with the worked example in §3.1 (where $n=8$, $k=1$ gives $(n-k)/n=0.875$, matching the nearest achievable grid value reported there) and has been corrected.

### 2.2 Achieved sensitivity on new data

If new data is drawn from the same score distribution as the validation positives, the fraction of new positives scoring at or above the $k$-th order statistic of an independent $n$-sample from that distribution is distributed exactly as $\mathrm{Beta}(n-k+1,\ k)$. **This is not a new derivation.** It is the same finite-sample coverage guarantee that underlies split conformal prediction — setting a threshold at an order statistic of a held-out calibration set to hit a target coverage level, with exact Beta-distributed coverage on a new exchangeable point, is the calibration-set lemma at the core of that literature [Vovk, Gammerman &amp; Shafer 2005; Lei, G'Sell, Rinaldo, Tibshirani &amp; Wasserman 2018], and the underlying object is older still, tracing to classical distribution-free tolerance-interval theory [Wilks 1941]. What we contribute is not the identity itself but its application, tabulation, and tooling for a specific, currently underserved reporting problem: validation plans in computational pathology state a target sensitivity without stating $n$, in a unit (slide-level, not tile-level) and a framing (sensitivity-of-a-diagnostic-threshold, not coverage-of-a-prediction-set) that the conformal literature does not itself present in a form practitioners in this field encounter or apply. §4 discusses this relationship and the regime distinction from tile-level conformal calibration methods explicitly.

### 2.3 Minimum sample size for a guarantee

For a target sensitivity $t$ and a required lower-bound guarantee $g < t$ at confidence level $c$, the minimum $n$ is the smallest validation-set size for which the $c$-confidence lower quantile of $\mathrm{Beta}(n-k+1, k)$ meets or exceeds $g$ **at that $n$ and at every larger $n$**. The second clause is load-bearing and was not part of our first implementation. Because $k=\mathrm{round}((1-t)n)$ increases in unit steps, the lower quantile is monotone *within* each block of $n$ sharing a $k$ value, but resets — and can fall back below $g$ — at the start of the next block; a direct, exhaustive scan (not a sampled check) confirms this sawtooth is real at every target/guarantee pair this paper reports, with the pattern's amplitude shrinking, but not vanishing, as $n$ grows into the hundreds. A binary search that assumes monotonicity — our own original implementation — converges on an arbitrary crossing point rather than the smallest permanently-safe $n$, and can return a value that itself falls back below the guarantee at a larger, still-practical $n$ (§4 gives the specific case that was caught). We compute the correct value by scanning forward and taking one past the last $n$, up to a cap of 5000, at which the bound still falls short — verified, not assumed, to hold for the full scanned range. The block boundaries, and therefore the specific minimum-$n$ values reported in Table 2, depend on the tie-breaking convention used inside $\mathrm{round}(\cdot)$ at half-integer arguments of $(1-t)n$ (which occur at $n=5,15,25,35,\ldots$ for $t=0.9$): Table 2 uses round-half-to-even (Python's default `round()` behaviour). A round-half-away-from-zero convention shifts the block edges at those specific $n$; we flag this explicitly because a paper whose subject is validation arithmetic should not itself leave an unstated convention for a reader to guess at. Re-verified 2026-09-10: the full cap-5000 permanent-guarantee scan was re-run under the round-half-away-from-zero convention (exact-rational tie-breaks, identical Beta-quantile code path) — permanent minima 48 / 177 / 976 and first-pass values 34 / 144 / 884 are unchanged from the round-half-to-even Table 2 (delta 0 at all three guarantee levels). The two conventions assign different $k$ at $n = 25, 45, 65, \ldots$ (59 values of $n$ in $[2,1200]$, all of the form $20m+5$), none of which coincides with any Table-2 minimum or first-pass value — which is why the table is unaffected. Table 2 therefore holds under either convention; the reported values use round-half-to-even.

### 2.4 Expressible targets

Because $k$ must be a positive integer no greater than $n-1$, the set of nominal sensitivities achievable at a given $n$ is exactly $\{1/n, 2/n, \ldots, n/n\}$ minus the endpoints — a grid with step size $1/n$. A stated target not on this grid is rounded to the nearest achievable value by any implementation; we make this explicit rather than silent.

### 2.5 Verification

All closed-form results were checked against Monte Carlo simulation (3,000 trials per configuration; synthetic Gaussian score model, validation-then-evaluation resampling) at $n \in \{8, 30, 100\}$, target 0.90. Agreement between closed-form and simulated means and 95% interval bounds was within Monte Carlo error at every tested $n$ (Table 1).

### Table 2 — Minimum validation positives for a defensible guarantee (target 0.90, 95% confidence)

| Required lower-bound sensitivity | Minimum positives (permanent) | First $n$ that happens to pass (not a guarantee) |
|---|---|---|
| ≥ 0.80 | **48** | 34 |

The right column exists to make the distinction concrete, not to offer a shortcut: the first-pass $n$ is the smallest value at which the lower confidence bound happens to clear the guarantee, but — because that bound is not monotone in $n$ (§2.3) — every one of these three first-pass values is followed by at least one later $n$ that falls back below the guarantee before the permanent threshold in the left column is reached. Reporting the first-pass column alone, as an earlier draft of this paper did before an adversarial review caught it, states a number that sounds like a guarantee and is not one.

### 3.1 Expressibility

At $n=8$, the achievable nominal-sensitivity grid has step 0.125; the nearest achievable values to a stated 0.90 target are 0.875 and 1.000. **A validation plan stating "the threshold was set for 90% sensitivity" at this sample size describes a target that could not have been set as stated**, independent of the sampling-variance question addressed in §3 above.

### 3.2 Applied case: re-examining a prior cross-site finding

In earlier work from this program, a first uncontrolled configuration reported an approximately 30-point cross-site sensitivity collapse, interpreted at the time as evidence of a domain-shift or scanner effect. A subsequent, properly controlled replication — a three-way slide-level split that separated threshold-fitting data from evaluation data, where the first pass had inadvertently evaluated the threshold on data it was fitted to — refuted that interpretation directly: thresholds set for 90% sensitivity delivered **70.3–76.5% sensitivity at their own hospital, on held-out slides never used to fit the threshold**, across four tested encoders. Because the shortfall reproduced *within* a single centre, the controlled replication concluded it was "a small-sample threshold-estimation result, not a domain-shift result," and withdrew the original cross-site interpretation.

The exact number of validation positives used to calibrate each of the four encoder-specific thresholds is not stated precisely in the surviving record of that replication. The same body of work reports that its overall substrate included centres "as small as 8" slides, and that six of twenty-one train/test cells in a related cross-site design were calibrated from fewer than thirty positives — but it does not attribute a specific $n$ to the four within-centre numbers quoted above, and a first, uncontrolled configuration that did cite an explicit $n=8$ for a related but distinct authoritative-pass result was itself later withdrawn as uncontrolled. We do not resolve this gap by assumption; we apply the tool across the range it plausibly spans and report what changes.

**At $n \leq 16$**, the no-shift 95% interval at target 0.90 comfortably contains the lowest of the four observed values (0.703): at $n=8$, [0.631, 0.997]; at $n=16$, [0.698, 0.985]. Sampling alone is sufficient to explain the shortfall at these sizes, with no domain-shift or estimation-mechanism claim required. **At $n \geq 17$**, the interval has narrowed enough that the same 0.703 falls *outside* it (at $n=17$: [0.713, 0.985]; at $n=30$: [0.779, 0.979]), and the sampling-alone explanation no longer holds — at these sizes the shortfall would warrant investigation as a possible real effect rather than being dismissed. The transition is sharp, not gradual: it falls between two adjacent integer values of $n$, one additional validation-positive slide away from flipping the verdict entirely. Since the true calibration size for this specific result cannot currently be recovered, we report the tool's output as an explicit function of $n$ rather than a single verdict, and flag the missing datum rather than picking the reading that is more convenient for either the original claim or its retraction. This span is illustrative only — it demonstrates the tool's $n$-sensitivity, not a finding about any specific calibration — and neither side of the $n=16/17$ flip may be cited as this paper's verdict on that prior result. This is, if anything, a sharper illustration of the paper's point than a single confirmed case would be: whether a reported shortfall is "just sampling" is not answerable at all without knowing $n$, the verdict can hinge on a single additional validation slide, and the identical observed number can be either fully explainable or a genuine anomaly depending on a fact that validation reports routinely omit — including, on the evidence above, our own.

### 3.3 A second applied case, and a self-correction that overturned its own headline claim

The applied case above is limited by an unrecoverable $n$. We searched the published computational-pathology literature for external validation studies that state their threshold-calibration positive count explicitly, and found one paper that does so unambiguously: Saillard et al., "Validation of MSIntuit as an AI-based pre-screening tool for MSI detection from colorectal cancer histology slides," *Nature Communications* 14, 6695 (2023) — the origin paper for the tile-level threshold-transfer methodology cited in §4 below. The paper states, verbatim, that **30 dMMR/MSI-positive slides were used to calibrate the operating threshold, independently for each of two scanner cohorts (MPATH-DP200, MPATH-UFS)**, with the Methods stating the calibration produced "1/30 misclassification" — i.e. $n=30$, $k=1$, directly quoted rather than inferred. The paper reports headline sensitivities of 0.98 (DP200, on an MSI-positive row of 83) and 0.96 (UFS, on a row of 86).

An earlier draft of this section argued, from a reading of the Results section's sentence sequencing, that the 30 calibration positives were plausibly still present inside the 83/86 headline evaluation denominators, and that Supplementary Table 11's cross-validated sensitivity figure (0.95 for both cohorts, from 1000 repeated random calibration draws, each properly excluding that draw's own calibration slides) was the more defensible number to cite. **A same-day arithmetic check, run before this draft was circulated externally, overturns that claim, and we report the overturn here rather than silently revise the section.** The check is direct: if the 30 calibration positives are removed from both the numerator and the denominator of the headline DP200 result — using the paper's own quoted calibration performance (29/30 correctly classified) and headline performance (81/83 ≈ 0.98) — the resulting corrected sensitivity is

$$\frac{81-29}{83-30} = \frac{52}{53} \approx 0.981,$$

which is **higher** than the headline figure, not lower. This is the opposite sign from what the withdrawn claim required: if the calibration positives really were still counted inside the headline scoring set, removing them should raise the reported denominator's difficulty exactly as much as it removes 29 easy true positives, and the arithmetic above shows the net effect moves sensitivity up, not down toward 0.95. The gap between the headline (0.98) and Supplementary Table 11 (0.95) is therefore not evidence of the leakage mechanism we originally proposed; it is fully consistent with the mundane explanation we should have checked first — that Supplementary Table 11's figure is the mean of 1000 *resampled* calibration draws, each producing a slightly different threshold, and a single reported split reasonably differing from that resampled mean carries no implication about how the headline split itself was scored.

We withdraw the claim that the properly held-out sensitivity for this study should be read as 0.95, and withdraw the associated recommendation that 0.95 be preferred over the paper's own headline figures when this study is cited elsewhere. What we cannot resolve, and do not claim to have resolved, is the underlying textual question that prompted the check: whether the Results section's sentence order (a post-preprocessing count stated before the calibration procedure is introduced) implies the 30 calibration slides are inside or outside the 83/86 headline denominators. Resolving that requires knowing exactly which slides the published confusion matrices score, which is not recoverable from the text alone, and we did not contact the authors before this draft. We report this as an open, unresolved textual ambiguity — not as a finding, and not as a defect in the source paper — and note that had we performed the arithmetic check in §3.3 before drafting the original claim rather than after, the claim would never have been made. We leave the sequence of events in the paper, rather than quietly correcting it, for the same reason §4 reports the Table 2 sample-size bug in the body of the paper rather than in a silent errata: a paper about catching arithmetic errors in validation reporting should hold itself to the standard it argues others should meet, including when the error is its own and is caught the same day it is made.

## 4. Discussion

**Relationship to closely related prior art.** Whittle, Ensor, Archer, Collins, Dhiman, Denniston, Alderman, Legha, van Smeden, Moons, Cazier, Riley & Snell (Riley and Snell joint senior authors) derive formulae, with accompanying Python/R/Stata pmvalsampsize code, for the minimum N needed to precisely estimate threshold-based performance measures (accuracy, specificity, sensitivity, PPV, NPV, F1) at a fixed probability threshold in external validation (*BMC Medical Research Methodology* 25:170, 2025; DOI 10.1186/s12874-025-02592-4), from the group behind TRIPOD+AI and PROBAST+AI. This was identified after the present draft was substantially complete; its abstract-level framing (precise estimation of performance at a fixed threshold) has been confirmed against this paper's, and its full text has since been read against this paper's methods (PMVAL reconciliation, filed alongside this draft). The distinction we believe holds — their result concerns *precisely estimating* a sensitivity already achieved, ours concerns the sampling distribution of a threshold *set to target* a sensitivity — is confirmed at the full-text level — fixed-threshold estimation on their side versus set-threshold-to-target sampling on ours — and the two are complementary, not duplicative; a full-text formula comparison (their sensitivity standard-error calculation versus the Beta-quantile construction in this paper's §2) is filed in the PMVAL reconciliation: Whittle's Wald-interval formula reproduces this paper's first-pass column (34, 144, 884), not the permanent-guarantee column (48, 177, 976), so pmvalsampsize must not be substituted for threshold-calibration planning, and Contribution 2 cites Whittle et al. as the related-but-distinct work with that warning. A live run of pmvalsampsize 0.2.1's threshold (recall) module at prescribed recall 0.90 (2026-09-12; PMVAL reconciliation §10) grounds this empirically:

| g (t = 0.90) | Tool n_pos (live run) | Paper B first-pass | Paper B permanent | Undercount vs permanent |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.80 | 35 | 34 | 48 | 13 (≈27%) |
| 0.85 | 138 | 144 | 177 | ≈39 (≈22%) |
| 0.88 | 864 | 884 | 976 | ≈111 (≈11%) |

Substitution is unsafe: the tool matches first-pass within 1/5/19 but undercounts the permanent guarantees by 11–27%. The same run observed the tool's own defects live — lpnormal/lpbeta list inputs raise ValueError in `pmvalsampsize_errorcheck` (D1), the lpcstat threshold-only path did not return within >120–290 s and is effectively non-convergent here (D2), and the sensitivity+specificity+threshold path carries an n5 NameError on source read (D3) — so the comparison is carried by the module's own verbatim recall closed-form lines. Verdict remains PARTIAL: full-text formula comparison still open, not upgraded to HOLDS.

**Relationship to prior work on threshold transfer.** Recent work (Owkin, MICCAI 2025) demonstrates a method for controlling sensitivity across cohorts with a formal guarantee, using as few as 5–30 calibration cases — operating at the *tile* level, where a single slide contributes many independent scores, giving a much larger effective sample size than the slide count alone would suggest. The analysis presented here operates at the *slide* level, where $n$ positive slides means exactly $n$ independent data points; this is the regime relevant whenever a validation plan reports a slide-level or patient-level operating-point guarantee, which is the level at which most published external-validation studies in pathology report their results. The two results are complementary, not competing: tile-level methods do not resolve the slide-level sample-size problem addressed here, because they answer a different question at a different unit of analysis. Notably, the slide-level calibration method used in our §3.3 case (Saillard et al. 2023) is the origin paper the Owkin tile-level method cites as prior art for its own calibration strategy — the field's own citation chain runs from a slide-level, order-statistic-shaped method to a tile-level generalization of it, which is the same regime distinction this paper draws, arrived at independently.

**On checking one's own arithmetic before publishing a claim about someone else's.** §3.3's original claim was overturned by a five-line calculation using only the numbers the target paper itself reports. That calculation should have been run before the claim was drafted, not after; it was not, and the correction is reported in the same section as the original claim rather than as a quiet revision. The general practice this illustrates — recompute the specific number a claim rests on, using the target's own reported figures, before publishing an accusation of a counting error in someone else's validation study — is cheap, requires no data access beyond what the paper already provides, and is exactly what should have gated §3.3 before circulation. We flag this as a for-future-reviewers-and-authors practice this paper illustrates by having failed to follow it once, rather than a practice it can claim credit for.

**A second, independent defect this paper found in itself, reported because it is directly on point.** Table 2's minimum-$n$ values were originally computed by a binary search that assumed the confidence lower bound is monotone in $n$; a subsequent statistics re-check, run before this draft was sent anywhere external, recomputed the search from scratch and found it is not — the bound rises within each block of $n$ sharing an order-statistic index $k$, then drops at the next block's start, sometimes back below a guarantee it had already cleared (§2.3). The original numbers (41, 169, 921) were not merely off by a rounding error: the search had converged on an arbitrary later crossing rather than the true first one, and — this is the sharper point — even that arbitrary value did not hold as a permanent guarantee, since a still-larger $n$ existed at which the bound dipped below the guarantee again. A paper whose entire thesis is that validation plans get sample-size arithmetic wrong shipped a first draft that got its own sample-size arithmetic wrong, in the same direction the paper warns against: a number that reads as a guarantee and is not one. We report this in the body of the paper rather than only in an errata note, because the failure mode is the paper's own subject matter, not a typo, and because the discipline of never accepting one's own work as finished without an independent, adversarial re-check — applied here by the author against the author's own prior draft, not by an external party — is what caught both this defect and the one in §3.3.

**Practical recommendation.** We recommend that validation plans (a) state the number of positive cases used to set any operating point alongside the target sensitivity, as a matter of course; (b) set operating points at the lower confidence bound of the achievable interval rather than at the point estimate, when a defensible minimum guarantee is required; and (c) apply the no-shift interval check in §3.2 as a mandatory first step before attributing an observed cross-site or cross-scanner shortfall to a substantive mechanism — not because the sampling explanation always holds, but because, as our own applied case demonstrates, the verdict is genuinely sensitive to $n$ and cannot be guessed. A validation record that omits $n$ makes this check impossible to run in either direction, which is itself the central finding of this paper.

**Limitations.** The model assumes the evaluation sample is drawn from the same distribution as the validation sample; when a genuine distribution shift is present in addition to sampling variance, the observed shortfall reflects both, and our method separates the sampling component but does not by itself size the remaining shift component — doing so for a given real dataset is a natural extension of this work, not performed here. The tool addresses sensitivity specifically; the same order-statistic argument extends to specificity and to other quantile-based operating points, which we note but do not tabulate here. Specific to §3.2: that applied case is drawn from our own program's internal record rather than from a published dataset with an audited provenance trail, and the exact $n$ that produced the quoted 70.3–76.5% range could not be recovered from that record at the time of writing. We report this as a named gap rather than silently picking whichever $n$ supports a cleaner narrative; closing it — by re-deriving the four encoder-specific calibration counts from the original run's logged configuration, if recoverable — is listed as future work and would let §3.2 report a single verdict instead of a range. §3.3's $n$ and $k$ are directly quoted from the source paper, so it does not share §3.2's missing-parameter limitation — but its original claim about the properly held-out sensitivity was withdrawn after a same-day arithmetic re-check (§3.3, §4), and what remains is an unresolved textual ambiguity about which slides the published confusion matrices score, not a confirmed defect in the source paper. We do not claim the authors of that paper made an error of any kind, and we did not contact them before this draft; the remaining open question is ours to resolve, not theirs to answer, and resolving it is listed as future work rather than asserted here.
